AAC Fast Fact #1 · Activity 1.1

Sound vs. Sense

Speech, language, and communication are not the same thing. Here's how to feel the difference in about five minutes.

Why this activity exists. AAC is often introduced with "don't worry, it won't stop speech from developing." That's true, but it's not the point. The bigger point is this: a student who isn't talking yet may still have a rich language system underneath, and that system deserves to be taught and used right now, not held back until speech arrives.

Before that idea will stick, you need to feel the difference between producing speech sounds and understanding or building meaning. The four short tasks below will do that. No right answers are required to move on — the reveals are the point.

Task 1 of 4

Read this sentence aloud.

It's written in Japanese, transliterated into the alphabet so you can sound it out. Tap each piece, left to right, to build it up — then have your device actually say it.

Tap the first tile to begin…

You just read it correctly. This says: “Kore o yomeru nara, tachiagatte kudasai” — but hold that thought. Don't translate it yet. Notice only this: your mouth (or your screen reader) just produced accurate, fluent speech sounds. That's it. That's all speech is — motor output. Keep going to Task 2.

Task 2 of 4

Now, what did you just say?

Pick the meaning you think is most likely correct. Go with your gut — there's no way to actually know, and that's the whole exercise.

The real translation: “If you can read this, please stand up.”

Whether you happened to guess right or wrong doesn't matter. What matters is that you had zero real information to reason from. You decoded the sounds perfectly in Task 1. You could not touch the meaning in Task 2. Speech production and language comprehension are two different systems — you just watched them come apart.

Task 3 of 4

Now flip it. Find meaning with no real words at all.

Read this sentence. Three of its "words" are made up. Move each one to where it belongs.

“The glorp daxed the flinder before it splinked into the gard.”

One more: which happened first — the daxing, or the splinking?

You got real information out of a sentence with no real content words. Word order, the ending on "daxed," and the word "before" all carried structural meaning — grammar — independent of any actual vocabulary. Grammar can run without meaning underneath it (Task 3). Meaning can also exist without clear speech on top of it (Task 1 & 2, in reverse). They are separable. That separability is the entire argument for teaching language early, through AAC, regardless of whether speech is present yet.

Task 4 of 4

The takeaway

Speech

The motor act of producing sounds with the mouth, breath, and voice. It's output. Task 1 showed you can do this fluently with no comprehension attached.

Language

The rule system underneath: vocabulary, grammar, and meaning. It can be expressed through speech, writing, signs, symbols, or a device. Task 3 showed it can operate without real speech content at all.

A student who isn't talking is not automatically a student without language. Waiting to see if speech develops before building language and communication skills gets the two systems backwards. AAC gives language a way out that doesn't depend on speech ever arriving.

Reflection: In two or three sentences, explain to a colleague why AAC targets language, not just speech. Use what you just experienced in Tasks 1–3.
